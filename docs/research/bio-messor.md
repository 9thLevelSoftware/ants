**Genetic caste determination (important, unusual).** In Iberian *M. barbarus* two cryptic mitochondrial/nuclear lineages (Mbar1, Mbar2) coexist. **Workers are inter-lineage hybrids; new queens come only from within-lineage matings** ("social hybridogenesis"). Mother queens mate with ~2.4 males on average (observed mating frequency 2.41 +/- 0.16 s.e.; effective frequency 2.24 +/- 0.16; both inferred from worker genotypes, 27 colonies at Sorbas). The two lineages are at about equal frequency (nuptial-flight sexuals 20 Mbar1 : 22 Mbar2 at Baul). If mating were random, ~13.4% of queens (authors' estimate, derivation only in their supplement) would fail to mate with males of both their own and the other lineage. [S] [V] Norman et al. 2016, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5134035/ . Inference **[E]**: a queen that mated only with her own lineage could not produce workers; one that mated only with the other lineage could make workers but no gynes.

## 3. Colony founding

**Mode: independent, claustral, single foundress (haplometrosis).** Colonies are monogynous; the queen seals herself into a small chamber and rears the first brood from body reserves, no foraging. [S-weak] vendor care sheets above; monogyny: Norman et al. 2016 (above) and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messor .

| Step | Detail | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Nuptial flight in autumn (section 10), mating (queen ~2.4 males), dealation | [S] |
| 2 | Queen walks to soft, bare, sunny soil, digs a short shaft + chamber, plugs it | [S-weak]; depth **[E]** 15-30 BU (10-20 cm) |
| 3 | Lays first clutch, tends brood without eating (claustral) | [S-weak] |
| 4 | First workers (nanitics, minor-sized) emerge after 56-124 d; they open the plug, start foraging for seeds + arthropod protein | [S-weak]; nanitic count **[NF]**, **[E]** 5-10 |
| 5 | Slow growth to ~30-50 workers, then faster with first majors | [S-weak] https://www.antantics.wales/product-page/messor-barbarus-harvester |

Mortality: [NF] for *M. barbarus*. Analogue: in *P. barbatus* at the Rodeo NM long-term site only ~10-50 of very many founding queens survive to age one and survival depends on rainfall and neighbour foraging area (search summary of https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10316491 ; **not verified, [U]**). The earlier claim that claustral founding in *Pogonomyrmex* costs the queen ">50% of body mass" is **[U]**: the cited PMC2803755 (Enzmann and Nonacs 2010) is a laboratory study of incipient nest digging in *P. rugosus*, *P. salinus*, *P. californicus* and reports nothing on mass loss; it does say soil moisture and larger foundress size raise founding survival and that claustral foundresses dig deeper and close the nest more fully [V]. **[E]** for game: founding success 40% on Naturalist difficulty, determined by chamber site (soil softness, shade, neighbour distance) and weather.

## 5. Nest

**Substrate:** dry, open, sunny, well-drained soil: field margins, steppe, cereal fields, road verges, gravel and sandy ground. Seeds are not collected if buried deeper than ~1.5 cm (Hensen 2002, citing Bernstein; Schoening), so open ground with surface seeds matters. [S] https://revistas.um.es/analesbio/article/download/31421/30541/144521

**Architecture** (genus level; species depth data [NF]):
- A main shaft descends from several entrance holes; chambers and lateral galleries branch off; some chambers are brood rooms, others become **granaries** (https://antscout.web.app/species/messor-barbarus , [S-weak]). *M. arenarius*: one excavation reached ~2 m depth with first granaries/brood chambers near 2 m, spanning ~10 m2 (thewildmartin.com, [S-weak], single nest of another species). Keeper claim of 2-5 m is unsourced.
- Closest quantitative analogue: *Pogonomyrmex badius* granaries "usually between 40 cm and 100 cm below ground" (the paper's own burial experiment notes "in natural nests, seeds are stored below 30 cm"), stores of >300,000 seeds and >0.5 kg in one nest, nest gradients that "may exceed 2 meters" (Tschinkel and Kwapich 2016 [V], https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0166907). Larger *P. barbatus* nests reported to 4.5 m with hundreds of chambers (anecdotal, https://backyardnature.net/n/a/harvest.htm).
- Tunnel networks of *Messor sancta* become more meshed with more diggers (Gautrais et al. 2014, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4198125); useful as a rule for the automatic nest architect.

## 6. Diet and economy (the resource chain)

**Diet:** nearly exclusively **seeds** and fruits, from the soil surface and cut directly off plants (pre-dispersal predation of *Stipa tenacissima*, *Anthyllis*, *Helianthemum*, etc.). Dead arthropods are only 1.82% (*M. bouvieri*) and 2.59% (*M. timidus*) of food items carried home in two sympatric Messor (Hensen 2002 Table 4 [V], https://revistas.um.es/analesbio/article/download/31421/30541/144521). Seeds are protein-poor; queen and larvae need protein (keeper sources, [S-weak]), so arthropod carrion is a small but **critical** input **[E]**.

**Seed selection [S unless noted]:**
- Seed mass taken: 0.08 mg (*Thymus hyemalis*) to 95.9 mg (*Retama sphaerocarpa*, the heaviest seen entering a nest) (Hensen 2002 [V]); preferred 4-50 mg regardless of density (Detrain and Pasteels 2000 via Plowes 2013 [V]); seeds <0.4 mg "rarely selected" (Wikipedia summary [V], but note Hensen recorded Messor carrying 0.08 mg Thymus seeds, so 0.4 mg is a soft preference edge, not a hard floor). Hensen found **no resource partitioning by seed size** among the Messor species.
- Selection also by seed **length** (time-saving, not energy-optimising): longer seeds are found sooner and are less often buried (Azcarate et al. 2005 via Plowes 2013).
- Selectivity increases with trail length, not seed density (Detrain et al. 2000).
- On a trail, size-matching of ant to item weakens with distance from nest (Reyes-Lopez and Fernandez-Haeger 2001).
- Workers choose smaller pieces when same food is offered in several sizes (Heredia and Detrain 2005).

**Chain (game model):**

1. **Seed rain / standing crop** (plants: availability peaks shortly before summer drought, Hensen 2002).
2. **Harvest:** minors carry small seeds, majors take large/preferred ones, media lay trails and do most foraging (Heredia and Detrain 2000, https://repositorio.uc.cl/dspace/items/6166269b-95fc-44dd-9e89-53ef398e579c/full ; Wikipedia summary).
3. **Entrance triage:** chaff, husks, awns, glumes dumped at the midden; of returning collectors 57.0% (*M. bouvieri*) and 61.5% (*M. timidus*) were loaded with anything, and 49.6% / 57.7% carried food items, the rest empty or carrying plant bits (Hensen 2002 Table 4 [V]; N = 3100 ants).
4. **Granary storage:** damp-cool chambers, dozens of cm to ~m deep.
5. **Processing:** large-headed workers crack/mill hard seeds; chewing + saliva yields **"ant bread"** (Goetsch, as cited by Tschinkel and Kwapich 2016); may mimic germination (malting) to free sugars. The paper notes the major-worker milling specialisation "has not been quantified". Treat as plausible but weakly evidenced; game uses it as a mechanic with explicit uncertainty.
6. **Feeding:** processed paste to larvae and queen by trophallaxis/placement; adults eat paste and liquids.
7. **Waste:** empty husks to midden; midden seeds are mostly empty (Euphorbia study: 2 intact vs 185 empty seeds from 9 nests).

**Intake rates (corrected):** Hensen counted returning workers for 3 min each hour on two colonies of each species (six occasions, 30 Apr - 17 Jun 1999) and extrapolated. Mean collecting runs per colony per day were **40,980 and 16,310 for the two *M. bouvieri* colonies and 23,690 and 23,670 for the two *M. timidus* colonies** (the earlier text wrongly gave 40,980 and 16,310 as one value per species). These are *M. bouvieri*/*M. timidus*, not *M. barbarus* [V]. Hensen gives no mean item mass; item masses are the plant dispersal-unit masses of Table 2 (Helianthemum 0.68 mg, Launaea lanifera 0.80 mg, Thymus 0.081 mg, Stipa tenacissima 9.03 mg, Anthyllis 2.69 mg). Weighting by the Table 4 diet shares gives a mean item of ~0.97 mg (*M. bouvieri*) and ~1.84 mg (*M. timidus*, which carries far more Stipa). With 50-58% of runs loaded with food, daily intake is **~7.8 and ~19.7 g/day for the two *M. bouvieri* colonies and ~25 g/day for each *M. timidus* colony [E, our arithmetic, assumes whole dispersal units were carried]**, i.e. a **~8-25 g/day** spread, central ~15-20 g/day. Activity is intermittent: the share of colonies active fell from 100% to 9-13% between late May and mid June (*M. bouvieri* 91% -> 73% -> 9%; *M. timidus* 100% -> 13% in the 9-10 June sample) although temperature did not rise (Hensen 2002 Table 3 [V]), so there is a **seasonal drought lull driven by seed supply/cues**.

**Colony consumption [E]:** adult at ~10 uW metabolic rate (assumed for a ~7 mg worker; the measured mean worker mass is 6.9 mg, Merienne 2020) with seed at ~17 kJ/g needs ~0.05 mg/day (10 uW x 86,400 s = 0.86 J/day; / 17 kJ/g = 0.05 mg, arithmetic re-checked); x3 for activity, brood growth, milling loss => ~0.15 mg per real worker per day => **~1.5 g/day** for 10,000 workers (range 1-2 g/day). Peak harvest (8-25 g/day, see above) over ~100-150 active days gives ~1-3 kg/yr, i.e. several times the ~0.55 kg a year of consumption, leaving a large margin for losses, sprouting and unharvested days. The 10 uW figure and the x3 multiplier are unsourced; this is a balance target, not a measurement.

**Activity rhythm and temperature:**
- Colonies activate only above critical soil temperature and humidity; microclimate predicts colony activation and intensity but **not** task allocation (Azcarate et al. 2007). Thresholds themselves **[NF]**.
- Mild temperatures (20-35 C) give peak activity in the three Messor studied (*M. barbarus*, *M. bouvieri*, *M. capitatus*; the 20-35 C range is stated for the group, not for *M. barbarus* alone); *M. barbarus* was **mostly nocturnal in summer** and has more active colonies in autumn, whereas *M. bouvieri* peaks in summer (Arnan et al. 2023, interview text, [V], https://blog.myrmecologicalnews.org/2023/05/17/thermal-physiology-foraging-pattern-and-worker-size-interactions-in-ants/ ). Larger workers have higher heat-tolerance thresholds in all species [V]; the further claim that foraging temperature did not change forager size distribution was **not found** in the page read [U].
- Sibling species in Almeria: collecting begins in the evening when soil surface <40 C, has an evening peak, a steady night level, a second peak just after sunrise, then stops as soil warms; soil temperature observed 12-42 C during collecting (13.8-41.5 C for *M. bouvieri*, 12.0-42.1 C for *M. timidus*; Hensen 2002 [V]).
- In the morning, as temperature rises, *M. barbarus* shortens its foraging column and shifts from long physical trails to alternative sources (Doblas-Miranda and Reyes-Lopez 2008, abstract only: https://bioone.org/journals/environmental-entomology/volume-37/issue-4/0046-225X(2008)37[857:FSQRTT]2.0.CO;2/Foraging-Strategy-Quick-Response-to-Temperature-of-Messor-barbarus-Hymenoptera/10.1603/0046-225X(2008)37[857:FSQRTT]2.0.CO;2.full).

**Game activity curve [E, bracketed by the above]:** surface-soil temperature T: activity factor 0 at <=8 C, linear to 1.0 at 20 C, 1.0 to 35 C, linear to 0 at 42 C. Humidity gate: colonies stay in when surface RH/soil moisture below a colony threshold (rain days boost, deep drought suppresses).

Continuous size means weak but real size-matching: items and ant size correlate on trails, less so far from the nest.

**Caste ratio [NF]; [E] at maturity: minor 35%, media 45%, major 20%.** A measured worker size sample is available: mean worker mass 6.9 +/- 5.1 mg in a French colony with a right-skewed distribution (Merienne 2020, n = 430), which is consistent with many small/medium and few large workers but gives no class fractions. Majors appear after the colony passes ~30-50 workers (antantics.wales vendor page [V]).

**Task allocation (borrowed principle, species-adapted):** see section 15. Task split by age is plausible (young inside, old outside) but not documented for *M. barbarus*; Gordon's *P. barbatus* shows nest-maintenance workers are younger and safer than patrollers and foragers.

## 10. Seasonal cycle and nuptial flights

| Season (game days) | Calendar | Colony state |
|---|---|---|
| Spring 1-7 | Mar-May | brood boom as soil warms; trails reopen; seed rain from annual plants; foraging mostly diurnal-crepuscular |
| Summer 8-14 | Jun-Aug | drought lull in seed supply; foraging shifts nocturnal/early morning; granaries full; colonies may go quiet for days (Hensen 2002) |
| Autumn 15-21 | Sep-Nov | peak activity (Arnan 2023); **nuptial flights**; granaries restocked from autumn seed rain |
| Winter 22-28 | Dec-Feb | cold: reduced activity, brood largely arrested, trails persist unused; flights can extend into December |

**Hygiene and waste [S]:** non-food plant parts and empty husks go to a refuse pile (midden) beside each entrance. Midden soil is siltier, richer in K, organic matter and pH; seed bank density and richness increase greatly there; seedling richness and density are higher in refuse piles in autumn (Azcarate and Peco 2007 etc.; summaries: https://tourduvalat.org/en/publications-en/new-article-harvester-ants-as-ecological-engineers-for-mediterranean-grassland-restoration-impacts-on-soil-and-vegetation/print/). [S] https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0166907 ; Moggridge via https://antwiki.org/wiki/The_Ants_Chapter_18 (not fetchable here). No source found that *M. barbarus* dries seeds in the sun, and **granaries are often described as damp** for *P. badius* (a contested point: Wikipedia's Messor page says dry). Design stance: granary humidity is a **managed variable**, not a constant. *P. badius* actually relies on germination to open large seeds and feeds sprouts to larvae (74% of germinated seed equivalents in middens vs 2% of ungerminated), so radicle removal is **one** strategy in the guild, not universal.

## 12. GAME TRANSLATION

Calendar: 1 game day (gd) = 10 real min at 1x; a game year = 28 gd = 280 real min, so **1 gd represents 365.25/28 = 13.04 real (biological) days**; seasons of 7 gd (spring 1-7). Conversion: real days / 13.04 = gd (checked: 14 d = 1.07, 30 d = 2.30, 16 d = 1.23, 56 d = 4.29, 70 d = 5.37, 124 d = 9.51, 80 d = 6.13, 120 d = 9.20 gd). **Unit trap (added in verification):** durations (development, lifespans, founding) convert with /13.04, but the rows below that are "per day" rates (intake, consumption, g/day) are real **per-diel-cycle** rates. If the sim renders one diel cycle per gd while the stores and brood advance 13 real days per gd, the sim must either (a) multiply per-day flows by ~13 (net daily rate x 13.04 per gd), or (b) treat the diel cycle as an abstraction with the stock-and-flow ledger run in real-day units. Pick one in the TDD; the numbers in this table are written in real units (real day, real mass) and are not yet multiplied by 13.04.

### 12.1 Colony scale-down

Real mature colony: 8,000-12,000 (max ~21,000). **Sim cap: 1,500 workers** (scale k = 12,000 / 1,500 = 8 real per sim worker at the cap; at the typical mature sim size of 900-1,200 workers k = 10-13 for a 12,000-ant real colony, so use k = real colony size / sim colony size, not a constant 8). Rules that keep dynamics species-faithful:
- Flows (load size, consumption, brood food) scale with k so intake/consumption ratios match.
- Per-ant interaction rates (Gordon model) are **not** scaled; instead chamber volumes and entrance densities are scaled so contacts per ant per minute match the real field.
- Visuals: render all simulated ants; "background" real ants appear as ambient crowd density on trunk trails (shader instancing).
- Trail length cap in game: **1,500 BU (10.5 m)**, typical 150-700 BU (1-5 m); real trails reach 30 m but the 4000 BU (28 m) world would otherwise be crossed.

## 13. SIGNATURE MECHANICS

**13.1 Seed handling and size-matching.** Each seed item has mass, length, hardness, depth/burial and an elaiosome flag. A forager's size class sets which seeds it can lift, drag or cut. Inputs: item properties, forager size, distance-from-nest (size matching weakens with distance), colony stock level. Outputs: loads delivered, items abandoned. Failure modes: only tough large seeds available and no majors alive; only tiny seeds (below 0.4 mg) so labour is wasted. Player: possess a major to cut and haul a large seed, or place "forage here/avoid" markers to steer colony effort to larger-seeded patches.

**13.2 Trunk-trail network.** Trails are persistent, cleared and branched edges with a pheromone marker (Dufour) and physical clearing of obstacles. Colonies add branches after productive seasons and extend long links during scarcity (Lopez et al. 1994). Each nest entrance owns a trail tree; entrances can be linked by trails (1/3 in the field). Failure modes: trails blocked by objects (human events), neighbouring colony pheromone zones forcing detours, long links raising exposure to spiders and heat. Player: as media ant, clear a stone, lay a branch, or mark the end of a trunk trail to redirect; places "new entrance" marker to shorten a trail.

**13.3 Granary microclimate and germination clock.** Each granary chamber stores seed cohorts with moisture and a hidden germination clock that accumulates when moisture and temperature are high. Workers remove or gnaw radicles from sprouted seeds, move seeds to drier chambers after rain, or consume sprouts as larval food. Inputs: soil moisture at depth, flooding, chamber depth, worker allocation to granary care. Outputs: viable stock, rot, sprouted stock. Failure modes: flood after heavy rain, mould, tough seeds sprouting beyond use. Player: possess a minor/media to gnaw radicles, relocate seeds, or dig a drier chamber higher up; queen directives: "granary hygiene".

**13.4 Seed milling and "ant bread".** Majors (and large media) crack hard seeds and chew them with saliva into a paste (Goetsch via Tschinkel and Kwapich 2016) that is the main brood food. Processing may convert starch to sugar (malting hypothesis; flagged uncertain). Inputs: raw seeds, major availability, moisture. Outputs: paste ration, empty husks to the midden. Failure modes: no majors -> paste bottleneck even with full granaries; protein shortage stalls larvae even with ample paste. Player: possess a major to mill seeds; set diet emphasis (protein vs carbohydrate) via queen directives.

**13.4b Protein bottleneck (companion).** Arthropod prey is rare in the diet (1.8-2.6% of loads) but needed by the queen and larvae, so the player must keep a few foragers on carrion/prey and protect them.

**13.5 Thermal windows and diel shifts.** Forager activation depends on soil temperature, RH and colony state; summer shifts the activity to night; heat above ~40 C halts work; morning warming shortens trails (Doblas-Miranda and Reyes-Lopez 2008). Failure modes: heatwave starves colony; a cold-wet autumn delays flight; the colony is exposed to nocturnal predators when it shifts. Player: use "avoid/forage here" markers on a time-of-day schedule; play a night forager.

**13.6 Midden and seed-dispersal mistakes.** Every entrance has a chaff pile. Dropped seeds en route (depots on ~20% of trails) and unwanted intact seeds in the midden can germinate; seedlings alter local vegetation, shading trails or creating new food patches. Pre-dispersal cutting of seed heads depletes plants. Failure modes: midden choked with sprouts blocking the entrance; overharvested patch plants die out. Player: carry chaff out, or deliberately leave seed patches to regrow; observe the ecology in the scent/diagnostics view.

**13.7 Hybrid-lineage queens and autumn flights.** Each gyne and male carries a lineage tag (A or B). Workers can only be produced from A x B fertilised eggs; gynes from same-lineage fertilisation (Norman 2016). Inputs: lineage pool of males at the mating site (rain-front-triggered flight, 2-3 days after rain), number of mates (~2.4). Outputs: queen's worker production and gyne production ratio. Failure mode (inference): a queen that mates only with one lineage cannot make workers and the colony dies out ("13.4%" random-mating risk). Player: as a gyne, choose among mates (both lineages); as colony, time flights after rain; daughter colony inherits the lineage data.

**13.8 Interaction-rate task allocation (Gordon-style).** Workers carry a task state and a cuticular "signature". Entrance-chamber encounters set outgoing foraging: returning successful foragers raise a stimulus; patrollers come first and their return rate gates the foraging start; humidity/temperature scale the sensitivity (see section 15). Inputs: encounter rates by task, environment. Outputs: number of foragers out, midden workers, nest workers. Failure modes: the colony overreacts to disturbance, or stalls if patrollers die. Player: possess a patroller/forager, or disturb/seed entrance to see the stimulus network in the diagnostics view.

**13.9 Territory, spiders and intrusion.** Colonies partition ground by trunk trails; neighbour encounters trigger ritualised fights, majors rally via poison-gland alarm and stridulation. Zodarion spiders pick off lone workers on and beside trails (hunting mode and time of day **[E]**, only the diet specialisation is sourced); nest-invading beetles provoke a defensive crowd. Failure modes: losing a trail to neighbours; spider hot spots near trail ends. Player: possess a major to fight, build a granary-guard queue, or move the trail; protect minors; lizards/birds act as an additional scripted predator **[E]**.

**Gordon's interaction-rate model (summarised, since it informs colony AI regardless of species):**
- Colony-level task allocation (patrolling, foraging, nest maintenance, midden work) has no central control; individuals adjust tasks by the **rate of brief antennal encounters** with other ants and by cues sensed on contact (task-specific cuticular hydrocarbons; Greene and Gordon 2003 Nature 423: 32; summary https://sicb.org/?p=34412).
- **Patrollers** ("the colony's 30-50 patrollers act as gatekeepers for thousands of foragers": Greene and Gordon 2007, Am. Nat. 170: 943, abstract [V]; a 2025 review says the return of 10-30 patrollers can trigger a wave of hundreds of foragers, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12413562/ [V]) leave at first light; **their safe return** triggers foragers. If they do not return, foragers stay in. A return rate of ~**1 patroller mimic per 10 s** produced the highest foraging activity (Greene and Gordon 2007, Behav. Ecol. 18: 451 [V]; https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:beheco:v:18:y:2007:i:2:p:451-455; beads at 1 per 10 s also started foraging ~17.9 +/- 19.7 min sooner than blank beads). Patrollers also set the day's foraging direction by depositing Dufour secretions on a mound sector ~20 cm long (Am. Nat. 170: 943 [V]; https://doi.org/10.1086/522843). All of this is *Pogonomyrmex barbatus*, not *Messor*.
- **Foragers** (thousands) leave in response to the rate of returning **food-carrying** foragers; a model of this closed loop uses leaky stimulus k = 0.3, decay tau = 0.41 (also a = 0.35, eps1 = 0.2, eps2 = 0.05) and an FHN excitable-unit threshold (spike counts as a departure when v > 0.75) [V]; sensitivity ("volatility" c) increases after a forager's first trip and depends on temperature and humidity (Pagliara, Gordon, Leonard 2018, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6294393/). Field: the effect of returning foragers on outgoing foragers **increases with humidity (dew point)** (Gordon, Dektar, Pinter-Wollman 2013, PLoS ONE; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3662670).
- Observed steady-state outgoing rates ~0.25 ants/s (one colony) and ~0.65 ants/s (another colony) on the same day (two colonies only); recordings ran from the start of foraging in early morning until ~noon, and the paper states foraging "declines to no activity during the heat of the afternoon" (Pagliara et al. 2018 [V]; the Ingram 2016 review says foragers forage "until mid-afternoon").
- **Midden work / nest maintenance:** an ant's time at the midden correlates with how many midden workers it met; others switch to midden work when encounter rate per minute with midden workers is high (Gordon and Mehdiabadi 1999, https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s002650050573). Nest maintenance workers outlast foragers, which outlast patrollers (Gordon and Hoelldobler 1987; **not re-verified**, primary not accessible); younger workers do brood care, then maintenance, then foraging (Ingram et al. 2016, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5013789).
- Colonies differ in foraging rate and in their response to humidity (17 colonies, 94 trials, 3 years; Gordon et al. 2013 abstract [V]); the "consistent over years" part is from the earlier summary, so a persistent per-colony "temperament" parameter is a reasonable design choice [E].
- Caution from Gordon 2007 (Nature essay in the brief, [S3]): species are not interchangeable; use this model as a framework with per-species parameters.

**Recommendation: KEEP *Messor barbarus* as the seed-harvester playable species.**
1. It is the best-studied *group-foraging Old World harvester* (trunk trails, polymorphism, nuptial-flight data, spider enemies) and has the three castes needed for the seed-processing gameplay; *P. barbatus* has no major caste.
2. Its signature mechanics (granary germination control, ant bread, hybrid-lineage queens, Zodarion) are unique among the four launch species.
3. Gaps (nest depth, founding numbers, thresholds) are filled with labelled estimates and *P. badius* (Tschinkel) analogues.
4. **Use *P. barbatus* as a data donor, not a playable**: adopt the Gordon interaction-rate model (patroller-gated foraging, entrance valve, humidity-scaled sensitivity, task-by-encounter switching) as the **shared colony task-allocation engine**, with per-species parameters (including for *Lasius niger*).
5. Habitat caveat: *M. barbarus* is Mediterranean/dry; in a generated locality it should nest on dry, bare, sunny ground (lawn edges, gravel, sandy playground, pavement verges) and its seed economy needs a seed-bearing plant layer in the world generator. If a temperate-locality fit proves better, *Messor structor* (central Europe) is the fallback within the same genus, but its documentation is thinner **[NF in this research]**.
